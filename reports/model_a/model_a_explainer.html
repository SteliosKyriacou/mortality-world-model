Mortality world model · Model A · status 5 October 2026

A latent stochastic differential equation that learns how people age

Open-source research code: github.com/SteliosKyriacou/mortality-world-model. Every model, cohort and figure on this page is synthetic: the people are simulated from an aging equation we wrote, so the true answer is known. No real patient data have been used yet.

Summary

What it is. Model A describes a person's health as a point z moving through a 16-dimensional learned space. The point moves according to a stochastic differential equation: a learned drift (where people in your state tend to go next) plus learned, age-dependent random noise. An encoder turns a clinic visit, with whatever measurements it happens to have, into that point. A decoder turns any point back into markers such as HbA1c, CRP and blood pressure, and a separate head gives the death rate. This is the latent-ODE/SDE family of models1,2,3, applied to aging trajectories, close in spirit to earlier dynamical models of aging17,18,19.

Two versions, kept separate. They share the same dynamics model and differ only in the encoder. One is a masked transformer that reads each marker as a token8,9. The other is a heterogeneous graph neural network (HetGNN) that builds a small graph of markers, genes and pathways for each visit10,11.

How it was tested. On two simulated cohorts whose true equation contains six planted hallmarks of aging16, each paired with a null cohort that contains none of them:

  • 20,000 people with dense follow-up;
  • 500,000 people with UK Biobank-like follow-up29.

What it gets right:

  • Hallmarks. With 500,000 people, the transformer version detects all six planted hallmarks and none in the null cohort (one training seed each).
  • Treatment effect. Its average per-person treatment effect on HbA1c is within 4% of the true effect, and it partly identifies who gains survival from treatment.
  • Forecasting at 20k. It forecasts held-out people better than gradient-boosted trees.

What it gets wrong:

  • Survival. Its absolute survival is too pessimistic, by about 12–13 points at 15 years in every version. It ranks who dies first no better than a Cox model on baseline labs.
  • Forecasting at 500k. It falls slightly behind gradient-boosted trees.
  • Noise. It overstates how fast noise grows with age.
  • Long training. Trained for long, its drift slowly drifts away from the truth while its forecasts barely change: visits years apart cannot pin down fast dynamics.

The page lets you simulate held-out people under any treatment schedule and compare the model with the true equation.

Try it: simulate a person's future

This runs the trained networks in your browser: drift, landscape, noise, decoder and death-risk head, about 120,000 weights per model. Pick a held-out person, the model never saw them in training. Then add or remove treatment windows and the model simulates their futures to age 90. It uses the same Euler–Maruyama scheme5 as the model's own forecasts: 4 steps per year (training used 32 steps per visit pair). The JavaScript engine reproduces the Python model to within 0.05% on every network output, and full simulations agree to within Monte Carlo noise; webapp/test_engine.js checks this.

Because the world is synthetic, the page can also run the true equation from the same person's true hidden state under the same treatment schedule (dashed lines). The model starts from what its encoder made of the person's first visit; the truth starts from the exact state. Treated and untreated futures share the same random draws, so their difference is the treatment's effect alone.

What the treatment is. The model knows exactly one intervention: u, the synthetic nutrient-sensing treatment. In the true equation it halves the drift of the metabolic axis while it is switched on. Nothing else exists to add. In training, everyone treated was treated from their first visit onwards. Windows that start later or stop are therefore extrapolations: the true equation handles them exactly; the model has never seen one.

Population flow: the cohort as a fluid

The simulator above follows one person. Here a whole cohort moves at once: the 13,146 held-out people who entered the 500k world aged 40–45. Each is a particle that follows Model A. Each carries a survival weight exp(−∫λ(z) da) that drains with the death-risk head. By the Feynman–Kac formula, this weighted particle cloud is a Monte Carlo solution of the Fokker–Planck equation with mortality as a sink:

∂p∂a=−∇·(fp)⏟drift+12∇∇:(ΣΣ⊤p)⏟noise−λ(z)p⏟mortality sink

A grid solver is impossible in 16 dimensions, so particles are the faithful method. The same equation, with mortality as a killing term, goes back to Woodbury and Manton's random-walk model of aging36 and Yashin's stochastic process model37. Here the drift, noise and sink are learned. The same cohort is run through the true equation from each person's true state. Observed survival (Kaplan–Meier with delayed entry26) and observed marker distributions come from the held-out data.

What it shows. The true equation, run the same way, reproduces observed survival exactly (97.3% against 97.3% alive at 55; 94.5% against 94.6% at 60). That validates the method. The model's sink is far too strong: between 45 and 60 it removes 15% of the cohort where 5.4% actually die, about three times too much. At the population level the survival miscalibration is larger than the per-person view suggested. Its marker distributions are centred correctly in mid-life but too wide, consistent with the overstated noise. Late in life the model's median inflammation falls behind: median CRP at 80 is about 2.5 mg/L in the model against about 4 observed and in the truth. A likely cause is the over-strong sink removing high-inflammation people too early, which changes who is left alive.

Toward a Navier–Stokes of aging: momentum

In Model A the speed and direction of aging are fixed by where you are: position determines velocity, as in the overdamped (Stokes) limit of fluid flow. Model A2 gives each person their own aging velocity v, a pace and direction that persists and relaxes through friction Γ:

dz=vdt,dv=[F(z,u)−Γ(z)v]dt+Σ(z)dW

The model is age-free, as in the autonomous variant: only the state, the elapsed time and the treatment enter. The starting velocity at a visit is inferred from the previous visit when there is one. With strong friction A2 reduces to Model A.

At the population level, averaging the particles over velocity gives fluid equations for the cohort's density ρ and mean aging-velocity field u:

∂tρ+∇·(ρu)=−⟨λ⟩ρ,∂tu+(u·∇)u=F−Γu−∇·Pρ+sink terms

Here the "pressure" P is the spread of individual paces around the local mean: how differently people in the same state are aging. People do not collide, so it is not a physical pressure. It is the closure the model learns.

Test world. Each person has a hidden pace on the age-core and metabolic axes that persists for about 5 years, with 50,000 people seen every 2–5 years. A matched null world has the same spread of 4-year changes but no persistence. The question is whether A2 recovers the momentum, and whether it beats first-order Model A only where momentum exists.

The two models and where they stand

Both versions use the same Model A dynamics: 46,193 parameters, identical networks. They differ only in the encoder that turns a visit into a point z. Each is trained and stored separately (models/model_a_transformer, models/model_a_hetgnn). The table lists every trained version the simulator can run, with its results on held-out people of its own cohort.

1. The idea in one picture

A clinic visit is a snapshot: a few dozen numbers, many of them missing, taken at an irregular age. Aging is the process that connects the snapshots. Model A splits the problem in two. First, compress each snapshot into a position z in a learned space. Then learn the equation of motion of z. The same pattern is used in fluid dynamics: describe the state, then learn the rule that moves it.

The equation has two parts. The drift is where the average person in your position at your age tends to go next. The diffusion is how unpredictable that next step is. Both are small neural networks. Because the equation runs in continuous time, the model can predict a person 1.4 years or 11.9 years out with the same machinery, which matters when visits are years apart and never evenly spaced.

2. The synthetic worlds

On real data you never know the true equation of aging, so you cannot check whether a model found it. We therefore built a world where we do know it. 20,000 simulated people are each born with a hidden 8-dimensional state at age 30. The state evolves under an equation we wrote, simulated in steps of 0.05 years until death or the end of follow-up. People are seen at a baseline visit between ages 40 and 70, then 0–3 follow-up visits 1–8 years apart. This gives about 33,000 visits, and about 8,600 people have two or more. At each visit the hidden state produces 33 clinical markers through a fixed noisy mapping, plus a 500-gene panel for a quarter of people. Deaths follow a hazard that depends on the hidden state.

The eight hidden axes each have a job. We then built two versions of the world. In hallmarks-ON, six hallmarks of aging are planted. In hallmarks-OFF, every one is switched off and replaced with a carefully matched version that lacks the hallmark. A test that fires on ON but not on OFF is detecting the hallmark, not some artefact of the pipeline.

The 500,000-person world (UK Biobank scale). It has the same equation, hallmarks and null version, but a visit structure modelled on UK Biobank29:

  • Visits: a baseline visit at 40–70, then 0–3 repeat visits with probabilities 0.76 / 0.19 / 0.045 / 0.005 (none / one / two / three), spaced 3–12 years apart.
  • Follow-up: 13–17 years.
  • Gene panel: 11% of people, rarely repeated.

This gives 110,616 people with two or more visits in the ON world and 116,786 in the OFF world, 13 times the 20k world's 8,600. The proportions follow public descriptions of UK Biobank: about 500k recruited, roughly 20k at the 2012–13 repeat visit, tens of thousands at imaging visits, and about 54k with Olink proteomics30. They are approximations, not a reconstruction of UK Biobank. Deaths during follow-up: 21.3% (ON) and 6.2% (OFF).

3. The model, exactly

3.1 The two encoders (trained first, then frozen)

Transformer encoder (400,976 parameters, src/mwm/encoders/tabular.py)8.

Each of the 33 clinical markers becomes one token: its standardised value times a learned vector, plus a learned marker identity. A missing marker gets a learned mask token and is excluded from attention, so nothing is imputed. The gene panel, when present, is projected into 4 extra tokens. Two transformer layers (width 64, 4 heads) pool the tokens into a 16-dimensional z.

HetGNN encoder (157,456 parameters, src/mwm/encoders/hetgnn.py). It builds a small graph for each visit from the measured values only:

  • Nodes: the visit itself; one node per measured clinical value; one per measured gene; one per pathway that has at least one measured gene.
  • Edges: clinical→visit, gene→pathway (membership), gene→visit, pathway→visit, and visit→clinical.
  • Message passing: two rounds, using one GraphSAGE layer per edge type10 (relation-specific weights as in R-GCN11, implemented with PyTorch Geometric12). Messages are summed across edge types, with residual updates.
  • Readout: z is read from the visit node. A missing modality is simply an absent node.

In the synthetic worlds, the gene→pathway membership given to the encoder is the planted pathway list. That partly hands the pathway test its answer, so for the HetGNN the null cohort is the meaningful check. On real data the lists would come from MSigDB/Reactome.

Training, identical for both encoders. Each encoder is trained as a masked autoencoder9:

  • Masked reconstruction: 30% of the observed markers are hidden and must be reconstructed, with weight 1 for hidden entries and 0.5 for visible ones.
  • Death hazard (weight 0.5): a piecewise-exponential hazard on z, held constant over each visit interval.
  • Gene-panel consistency (weight 0.5): keeps z stable when the gene panel is missing.

Reconstructing hidden markers rather than visible ones matters. A latent that copies its own inputs also copies their measurement noise, which the dynamics model would then mistake for fast biology. After 40 epochs the latent is whitened (zero mean, unit variance per dimension on training visits) and frozen. Neither encoder overfits within 200 epochs; the validation reconstruction loss keeps falling.

Why 16 dimensions?

It was a judgment call made before training, not a tuned value; nothing in the results so far proves 16 is right. The reasoning:

  • The truth has 8 dimensions, and 8 is the floor. Fewer than 8 and two independent processes, say inflammation and the metabolic drift, have to share an axis and their dynamics get mixed.
  • The encoder needs slack. The markers depend on the hidden state nonlinearly (squared terms for CRP, glucose, NT-proBNP, triglycerides and frailty index), and the encoder also has to carry the gene panel and the hazard. Extra dimensions let it lay these out without bending the 8 true axes. Twice the true size is a common rule of thumb for this.
  • The extra dimensions are cheap for the SDE. Model A has 46k parameters at 16 dimensions. The noise and drift networks scale with the latent size, but a 16-dimensional SDE trains in under two minutes.
  • Too many dimensions hurt. Every extra dimension is somewhere for encoder noise to go, and the SDE then has to explain it as diffusion. That makes the landscape harder to interpret (more rotation, less identifiable). On real data, where the true size is unknown, too much room is a real risk.

How well it worked: a linear map from the 16 learned dimensions recovers the 8 true ones with mean R² 0.82–0.87, depending on the version (Table 1). A sweep over 4–32 dimensions is scripted (scripts/train_model_a_long.py --d) but has not been run, so 16 remains a judgment call.

3.2 The equation of motion

dz=fθ(z,a,u)da+Σφ(z,a)dW,fθ=−∇Vψ(z)+Jθ(z,a,u)

Here a is age in years, u ∈ {0, 1} is the intervention flag, and W is Brownian motion. The drift has two networks:

Vψ
a scalar "landscape" over z (MLP 16→128→128→1, SiLU, 18,817 parameters). Its downhill direction is one part of the drift.
Jθ
everything else: age dependence, the intervention and rotational flow (MLP 18→128→128→16, 21,008 parameters; input [z, (age − 60)/10, u]). A small penalty (10⁻³‖J‖²) pushes as much of the drift as possible into the landscape. The split between V and J turned out not to be identifiable (§10); only their sum is meaningful.
Σφ
diagonal noise that depends on state and age (MLP 17→64→64→16, softplus output, 6,352 parameters), with a small penalty 10⁻³‖Σ‖².

In total Model A has 46,193 parameters. It is deliberately small: everything is in a 16-dimensional space. The structure, a learned SDE in the latent space of an encoder, follows latent ODE/SDE models for irregularly sampled data1,2,3,4.

3.3 How it is trained

Training data are pairs of consecutive visits from the same person: (z0, age a0) → (z1, age a1), giving 9,681 training pairs, 1,275 validation pairs and 1,281 held-out test people (seed 0). For each pair the model simulates K = 32 futures from z0 to a1. The interval is rescaled to s ∈ [0, 1] so that every pair, whatever its gap, takes 32 Euler–Maruyama steps:

zk+1=zk+f(zk,ak,u)Δah+Σ(zk,ak)Δahεk,h=132

The loss asks: how likely is the real z1 under a Gaussian fitted to the 32 simulated endpoints? Let μK and s2K be their mean and variance per dimension, and r2 the encoder's own measurement noise:

ℒ=∑d[(z1d−μKd)22(sKd2+rd2)+12log(sKd2+rd2)]+10−3‖J‖2+10−3‖Σ‖2

Why r is fixed, not learned (errors-in-variables13). We estimate r2 once. We perturb 4,000 training visits 16 times by each marker's measurement noise, as estimated by the decoder, and measure how much z moves (0.08–0.21 per dimension, i.e. 8–21% of the latent variance). The start state is jittered by the same amount during training (errors-in-variables). When we first let the model learn r itself, it explained the encoder's noise as fast, large biological noise. With visits at least a year apart, the data cannot distinguish the two.

Optimisation:

  • Common to every version: AdamW, weight decay 10⁻⁵, batch 512, gradient clip 5.
  • 20k versions: learning rate 2×10⁻³, cosine over 80 epochs, best validation likelihood kept (about 100 s and 5.6 GB on one RTX 4070 Ti).
  • 500k version: learning rate 5×10⁻⁴, cosine over 600 epochs. It adds a Jacobian penalty 0.01·‖∂f/∂z‖²F, estimated with Hutchinson's trick6,7, and a guard that rolls training back if the loss diverges. The checkpoint kept is the best validation forecast error (epoch 275; about 13 s per epoch).

Forecasts use 4 Euler–Maruyama steps per year (40 network evaluations for 10 years).

3.4 Training length, overfitting and the learning rate

All runs, with curves, are on the training-convergence page.

With 20,000 people:

  • Model A converges within 20–40 epochs.
  • From about epoch 150, training and validation loss separate, the signature of overfitting, and held-out forecast error and drift error rise with them.
  • On HetGNN latents the held-out metrics hold up longer (forecast error flat to about epoch 1,000), but the model still overfits.
  • The first long run held a high learning rate (2×10⁻³) for thousands of epochs and diverged to NaN at epoch 1,855.

With 500,000 people:

  • Training and validation loss stay together, so the overfitting was a data-size problem.
  • A slower problem remains. At a near-constant learning rate, the drift error against the truth climbed from 0.60 to 0.81 by epoch 900 and the learned treatment effect shrank from −0.019 to −0.011 (true −0.020), while forecast error stayed flat (0.691–0.693).
  • The cause: with visits 3–12 years apart, a slow drift and a fast pull-back plus large noise produce the same visit pairs.

None of the penalties helps. The noise and kinetic-energy penalties make the drift worse by suppressing real dynamics. What limited the creep was annealing the learning rate, and in practice, keeping the best early checkpoint. The real fix is data on short timescales: denser lab draws, or dated clinical events entered as source terms (§10).

3.5 How predictions are read out

A forecast for a person is 32 (or more) sample paths of z. Pushing each path through the decoder gives marker trajectories with uncertainty bands. Pushing it through the hazard head gives an instantaneous death rate λ(z(a)). Integrating that rate along each path and averaging over paths gives a survival curve. The decoder and hazard head are the frozen ones from the encoder stage. Model A only moves the point.

4. The aging landscape it learned

Sections 4–8 are the first, detailed validation: the transformer version, trained on the 20,000-person world for 80 epochs (runs/synthetic/{on,off}/seed{0,1,2}). The separated model_a_transformer was later retrained with the same settings, and its results are within seed-to-seed noise of these. Results at 500,000 people are in §9.

The drift is a vector field in 16 dimensions, which nobody can look at. To see it, we cut a 2D slice through the space and ask which way the field pushes within that slice. The horizontal axis is the model's own arrow of aging: the single direction w along which its drift points forward at almost every held-out state (§7.3). The second axis is the direction in z that best predicts the true inflammation axis, made perpendicular to w.

The picture of development or aging as a ball rolling through a landscape goes back to Waddington15; here the landscape is computed from the learned model. A field in a plane can be split into a downhill part and a swirling part. We solve for the height map U whose downhill direction best matches the drift in the slice (a least-squares Helmholtz projection). The surface below is that U; balls roll downhill on it. In this slice the downhill part explains 87% of the flow, and 13% is rotation that a surface cannot show. Model A's own landscape network Vψ is not used here, because it turned out not to be identifiable (§10).

The table decodes the index person's mean path back into clinical units at each milestone. Nothing here was observed; it is the model's forecast from one visit at 42.6. The "with intervention" column re-runs the same 96 futures, with the same random numbers, with u = 1.

What the intervention does to the index person

The model has one intervention it can apply: u, the randomised nutrient-sensing treatment planted in the synthetic world (it halves the drift of the true metabolic axis). In training, people who got it got it from their baseline visit. Here we switch it on for the index person at baseline (42.6), 55, 65 or 75 and simulate 256 futures per scenario to age 90. Every scenario reuses the same 256 random noise sequences. Each future with the intervention therefore has an exact twin without it, and the difference between the twins is the intervention's effect alone. Later starts are an extrapolation: the model never saw anyone start treatment mid-follow-up.

Because this world is synthetic, we can run the same scenarios through the true equation from this person's true hidden state (4,000 futures) and check the model's answer.

Reading the result. The model gets the metabolic effect right and specific. HbA1c and glucose bend down, starting at the moment of treatment and growing with time. By 90, treatment from baseline lowers HbA1c by a median 4.1 mmol/mol (80% of twins: 2.6–6.1). The non-metabolic markers barely move: their paired effect bands straddle zero, as planted. It also gets the order of benefit right: the earlier the start, the larger the gain. But it underestimates the survival benefit by about half: +0.45 years alive before 90 against +0.98 in the truth when started at baseline, and none at all for starts at 65 or 75 where the truth still gives +0.23 and +0.05. The cause is the hazard head, not the dynamics. It was fitted once on the encoder's latent and gives the metabolic direction too little weight. This is the same weakness that makes the model's survival predictions no better than a Cox model (§6). Training the hazard along simulated paths (§10) is the fix to try.

Intervention predictions vs reality

The scenarios above are for one person. Here we test the model's intervention predictions across the held-out cohort, against two kinds of reality.

Trial-style check. The treatment was randomised: 30% of people got it from their first visit. Held-out people therefore form a treated arm and an untreated arm, just as in a trial. We measure each person's actual HbA1c change per year between their first and last visit. The model predicts the same quantity for the same people from their first visit and their actual arm, and the true equation does too. We do the same for survival: the observed Kaplan–Meier curve26 in each arm, against the model's predicted survival for exactly those people. This is the check that can be run on real cohorts, wherever treatment was randomised or can be credibly treated as such.

Per-person check. Only possible in a synthetic world: for 400 held-out people, the predicted effect of treating from baseline against the true equation's effect for the same person.

Reading it. At the level of the two arms the model is right. The observed slowdown in HbA1c rise is −0.113 mmol/mol per year (95% CI −0.223 to −0.004). The model predicts −0.110 for the same people and the true equation gives −0.128. Each arm's own rate is also close: untreated 0.30 observed vs 0.28 predicted, treated 0.19 vs 0.17. Per person, the model has the direction right for everyone. Its average effect is 82% of the true one (−1.17 vs −1.42 mmol/mol at +10 years), and it spreads that effect across people (−0.77 to −1.54) where the truth is uniform. For survival, two problems show. Its absolute risk is too pessimistic: it predicts 67% alive at 15 years against 79% observed and 79% under the truth. Its per-person survival benefit does not track the true one (correlation −0.30). The ranking of who dies first is fine (C-index 0.78); the calibration is not. A likely cause: the model's noise grows too fast with age (§7.2), so simulated paths fan out too far, and averaging an exponential hazard over a wider fan raises it. These numbers are from the 80-epoch model; the overnight model will replace them.

Where it went wrong at 20k: the frailty basin

The same construction in the frailty plane shows the model's one clear miss. The truth (left) has two parallel valleys, robust and frail, separated by a ridge. People sit in one or the other, and the observed states are bimodal along this axis (below). Model A's landscape (right) has a single broad valley. It treats occasional jumps between wells as extra noise. The wells relax in about 0.3 years and visits are at least a year apart, so a model that only sees visit pairs never watches anyone settle into a well. Its Gaussian transition likelihood also cannot represent a two-humped landing distribution.

At 500,000 people this changes. The 500k transformer version finds two attractors, with excess deaths in the high-risk basin (one-sided Fisher p ≈ 3×10⁻¹⁷), and finds a single attractor in the null world. The landscape figure below still shows the 20k model.

5. Does it recover the truth?

We check this in two settings. In the oracle setting, Model A is trained directly on the true 8-dimensional hidden state, which isolates the dynamics model from the encoder. In the encoder setting, it is trained on the learned 16-dimensional latent, as it would be on real data. To compare the learned drift with the truth there, we fit an affine map from learned to true coordinates on training visits. That map recovers the true state with mean R² = 0.82 ± 0.04 (per axis 0.76–0.97), and we push the learned drift and noise through it. The error measure is normalised RMSE per true axis: drift error divided by the size of the true drift (0 = perfect, 1 = no better than predicting zero).

6. Forecasting real futures

The practical test: take each held-out person's first visit, predict their last visit (1–24 years later), decode it, and compare with the markers actually measured there. The baselines get the same inputs. They are last observation carried forward (LOCF); a per-marker linear trend in age; an MLP; and a gradient-boosted tree model (GBM) that sees the baseline markers, age, gap and intervention directly. Errors are in standard-deviation units of each marker, averaged over all observed markers.

7. Hallmark tests

Each test turns a hallmark of aging into a measurable property of the learned equation, with a decision rule fixed in prereg/analysis_plan.md. All tests run on held-out people only. Each is run on the true equation (it must fire on ON and stay silent on OFF), then on Model A trained on ON and on OFF, with 3 seeds each. Three control versions of Model A, trained on ON with seed 0, show which part of the model each detection relies on. Four tests were re-specified after the first synthetic run because their first versions failed on the true equation itself; §10 lists them.

7.1 Inflammaging 3/3 ON · 0/3 OFF

Question
Does the model predict that inflammatory markers rise slowly in mid-life and then accelerate?
Method
Take up to 400 held-out people aged 40–50 and simulate each to age 90 with u = 0 (16 futures each). Decode the mean path, and average the four inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, fibrinogen, WBC, standardised) into a composite. Fit its slope at ages 45–60 and at 72–87.
Rule
late/early slope ratio > 1.5 and bootstrap 95% CI lower bound > 1
Result
ON ratio 8.51 ± 0.33 (truth 7.19); OFF 0.01 ± 0.61 (truth 0.09). The model curve tracks held-out older people's measured values (correlation 0.98 above 60).

7.2 Loss of homeostasis (dispersion) 3/3 ON · 0/3 OFF

Question
Does unpredictability grow with age?
Method
Evaluate total noise tr(ΣΣᵀ) at the same held-out states, once with age set to 80 and once with age 45, so only age differs. Also fit the slope of log-noise on age, against an age-permutation null.
Rule
ratio > 1.2, CI lower > 1, slope > 0 with permutation p < 0.05
Result
ON 7.49 ± 0.44 (truth 4.43); OFF 1.00 ± 0.07 (truth 1.00). Direction right, magnitude overstated by about 70% (see Fig. 7b). The constant-noise control cannot detect it, by construction. The shuffled-age control does not detect it (0.97), so the detection genuinely depends on age.

7.3 Irreversibility 3/3 ON · 0/3 OFF

Question
Is there an "arrow of aging": a direction the model never runs backwards along without intervention?
Method
At 1,000 held-out states compute the drift f. Find the max-margin direction w that separates {f} from {−f} (linear SVM through the origin35) on one half. On the other half, count states whose drift points backwards (w·f ≤ 0). Then simulate 10 years and count mean paths that moved backwards along w.
Rule
backwards states < 10% and backwards 10-year paths < 5%
Result
ON 0.6% of states, 0–1.2% of paths (truth 0%, 0%). OFF 11–39% of states, 18–40% of paths (truth 15%, 14%).

7.4 Deregulated nutrient sensing 3/3 ON · 0/3 OFF

Question
Does the model know what the intervention does, and does it act on the metabolic system specifically?
Method
Take Δf = f(u = 1) − f(u = 0), averaged over 500 held-out states, and decode it into marker space through the decoder's Jacobian. Compute the share of the change that lands on the 8 metabolic markers. The null is 1,000 random latent directions of equal size, decoded the same way. Separately, simulate 10 years with u = 1 and with u = 0 using the same random numbers, and compare metabolic change.
Rule
metabolic share above null (p < 0.05) and the 10-year metabolic change slows (CI upper < 0)
Result
ON share 0.62 ± 0.03 vs null mean 0.24 (95th percentile 0.48), p = 0.003–0.015; 10-year effect −0.20 to −0.24 SD. OFF share 0.24–0.30, p > 0.34, effect slightly positive.

7.5 Molecular hallmarks via the Jacobian 2/3 ON · 0/3 OFF

Question
Do the directions in which the model ages fastest map onto the planted aging pathways in the gene panel?
Method
At 300 held-out states compute the drift Jacobian ∂f/∂z, take the 2 eigenvectors with the largest real part (the least stable, "aging" modes) and average them into one subspace. Decode it into the 500 genes through the decoder Jacobian, and score each pathway by the mean gene loading, a set-level enrichment in the spirit of GSEA21. There are two nulls: 2,000 random subspaces (direction null, Benjamini–Hochberg across 20 pathways22) and random gene sets of equal size.
Rule
≥ 1 planted aging pathway with q < 0.05 on both nulls, and ≤ 1 non-aging pathway detected
Result
Seed 0 finds 5 of 7 planted pathways (OXPHOS, UPR, DNA repair, mTORC1, IGF-1) with 0 false positives, matching the true equation exactly. Seed 1 finds 0 and seed 2 finds 2; OFF finds none. SASP and the inflammatory response are not found even in the truth: inflammation relaxes quickly (0.4/yr), so it is never a leading slow mode. The no-landscape and shuffled-age controls find nothing, so this test does rely on the landscape term and on age.

7.6 Frailty attractors 0/3 ON · 0/3 OFF

Question
Does the landscape have distinct basins, and do people in the high-hazard basin die more?
Method
Move 2,000 held-out baseline states onto a common slice perpendicular to the arrow of aging, then follow the drift within the slice until it stops. Cluster the endpoints into attractors. Assign each person to a basin and compare death rates between the highest- and lowest-hazard basins (one-sided Fisher test).
Rule
≥ 2 attractors holding ≥ 3% of states each, and excess deaths in the high-hazard basin with p < 0.05
Result
One attractor in every seed (truth on ON: 2 attractors, death rate 0.50 vs 0.16, p ≈ 3×10⁻²²). See Fig. 4.

7.7 Hazard attribution (encoder-level) 3/3 ON · 1/3 OFF

This test checks the encoder and hazard head, not Model A. It uses integrated gradients20 to attribute each person's predicted death rate to their input markers, then asks whether the inflammatory share grows with age. On ON it rises from 6.0% in the 40s to 9.4% in the 70s (slope 0.011 per decade, CI 0.009–0.013), above the same slope in a Cox model (0.004). It also fired on one OFF seed, so on real data it will only count when it beats the Cox comparison, as the pre-registration specifies.

10. What this does and does not show

Nothing here is about human biology yet. Both worlds are simulated, with hallmarks we planted. The results show the machinery can recover such structure when it exists and stays silent when it does not. Whether real cohorts contain it is the question for HRS, CHARLS and UK Biobank, with rodent transcriptomic data (TACO31) for the molecular hallmarks.

What the tests support

  • The tests are calibrated. On the true equation, all six hallmark tests fire on the ON world and none on the null world, in both cohorts. A positive result on real data would therefore say something about the data, not about the test.
  • The model can find planted mechanisms in data shaped like a real cohort: sparse, irregular, noisy, with deaths removing people. At 500k it finds all six hallmarks with no false alarms in the null world (one seed).
  • Treatment effects on the targeted system are recovered closely at 500k, both in trial-style arm comparisons and per person.

Known weaknesses

  • Survival is miscalibrated. In every version the model's 15-year survival is about 12–13 points too low. Its ranking of who dies first is no better than a Cox model on baseline labs, though in these worlds hazard depends only on the current state, so a trajectory cannot add information. A likely cause is noise that grows too fast with age, which inflates the hazard averaged along paths. The death-risk head is also fitted once on the encoder and never along simulated paths. Training it along paths is the next fix to try.
  • Fast dynamics are not identifiable from slow visits. With 1–12-year visit gaps, a slow drift and a fast pull-back plus large noise fit the data equally well. Long training slowly moves the model towards the wrong one, and no penalty we tried prevents this (§3.4).
  • Sudden events cannot be represented. The noise is continuous and Gaussian. Acute events such as a heart attack or stroke, which shift the state in days and then partly recover, are not in the synthetic worlds and cannot be expressed by the model. The planned extension is a jump-diffusion: dated clinical events enter as source terms with state-dependent rates, which the dated hospital records in UK Biobank make observable.
  • Noise growth with age is overstated (about 8× against a true 3.6–4.4× between ages 45 and 80).
  • The landscape network V is not interpretable on its own. Only the full drift is constrained by the data; the 3D surfaces on this page are Helmholtz projections of that drift, not V.
  • Forecasting at 500k is slightly behind gradient-boosted trees, and clearly behind on the 500k null world. Part of this is the encoder bottleneck: the learned latent explains 80–87% of the true state linearly.
  • Seed sensitivity. The pathway test varied across seeds at 20k, and the 500k results are a single seed.
  • Tests were redesigned after seeing results. Four hallmark tests were redesigned after the first 20k run, because their first versions failed on the true equation itself. The final rules are fixed in the pre-registration (prereg/analysis_plan.md) before any real data.
